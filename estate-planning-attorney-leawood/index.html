What should estate planning cover?

Estate planning sets out who can make decisions for you if you cannot, and how your property and responsibilities should be handled during your life and after your death. The right plan depends on your family, assets, and priorities, not on collecting the largest number of documents.

A plan may include a will, financial power of attorney, health care directive, or trust. These documents serve different purposes, and a will alone generally does not address every decision that may come up while you are living.

What might a local attorney check first?

Before recommending documents, an attorney may ask about your family relationships, the people you trust, your property, and any plans you have already signed. They may also ask about minor children, a family member with support needs, a business, or property in more than one state.

Bring existing documents and a general picture of your assets and accounts, but you do not need to prepare a perfect inventory before an initial conversation. A useful review also checks whether beneficiary designations and joint ownership fit the plan, since some assets may pass outside a will.

  • Who should handle financial or health care decisions if you cannot?
  • Who should receive your property, and are there conditions or timing concerns?
  • Do you need to name a guardian for minor children?
  • Are there older documents that no longer match your wishes?

Which documents or planning approach may fit?

A will can state how property should be distributed under the will and nominate a guardian for minor children. A power of attorney can authorize someone to handle specified financial matters during your lifetime, while a health care directive can record care preferences and name someone to make health decisions when permitted.

A trust may be useful for some families, but it is not automatically needed for every estate. Its terms, the property placed in it, and the way it is maintained all matter. An attorney can explain whether a trust addresses a specific goal or adds work without a clear benefit.

What can change the cost and timing?

There is no single fee that applies to every estate plan. The price may depend on which documents you need, how much customization is involved, whether your circumstances are straightforward, and how the attorney bills for drafting, revisions, and follow-up.

Timing can depend on how quickly you provide information, how many people need to review the plan, and whether the attorney needs to address complex assets or family arrangements. Ask for the proposed scope, billing method, what is included, and what could lead to additional work before signing an engagement agreement.

Questions to ask before you hire, a checklist

  • Who will meet with me and prepare or review the documents?
  • What documents do you recommend for my situation, and why?
  • What is included in the quoted scope of work?
  • How do you charge, and what work could add to the fee?
  • How long does a plan like mine usually take, and what could delay it?
  • How do you handle revisions if my wishes change before signing?
  • Will you explain how beneficiary designations and jointly owned property relate to the plan?

When should you call an estate planning professional?

Consider getting advice when you have a new child, marriage, divorce, significant change in assets, or a new responsibility for someone who depends on you. It is also sensible to review documents after a major life change or if they were signed many years ago.

A professional can be especially helpful if relatives disagree, you own property in more than one state, or you are unsure who should make decisions for you. If there is an urgent health or family situation, tell the attorney at the outset so they can discuss what may be possible and what still requires time.

What is the 5 and 5 rule in estate planning?

The 5 and 5 rule generally refers to a trust beneficiary's limited annual right to withdraw the greater of $5,000 or 5% of certain trust assets, depending on the trust terms and applicable tax rules. It is not a standard feature of every estate plan, so ask an attorney or tax professional whether it applies to your situation.

How long does it take to prepare an estate plan?

Timing varies with the number of documents, the complexity of your circumstances, and how quickly you can provide information and approve drafts. Ask the attorney for an estimate based on your needs and what could hold up signing.
