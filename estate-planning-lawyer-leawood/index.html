What should an estate plan cover?

Estate planning is about setting out instructions for your property and care if you become unable to make decisions, as well as what should happen after your death. A lawyer should start with your circumstances and explain which documents fit, rather than assuming everyone needs the same package.

A plan may include a will, a trust if it serves a purpose, financial powers of attorney, and health care instructions. It may also involve reviewing beneficiary designations and how property is titled. Those details matter because some assets pass according to their beneficiary forms or ownership arrangements instead of instructions in a will.

A useful plan should be clear enough for the people who may need to act on it. It should also be revisited after major changes, such as marriage, divorce, a new child, a move, or a significant change in finances.

What will a local lawyer review before recommending documents?

Expect a conversation about your family, the people you trust, your goals, and the kinds of property you own. The lawyer may ask who should handle financial matters, who should make health care decisions if you cannot, and whether you have concerns about a beneficiary receiving or managing an inheritance.

The review can include existing wills or trusts, account and insurance beneficiaries, real estate ownership, business interests, and property held outside Kansas. Bring what you have, even if it is old or incomplete. That gives the lawyer a chance to identify conflicts or gaps before drafting updates.

A lawyer should also ask about the people named to serve in important roles and whether they are willing and able to do so. Naming someone in a document is only part of the decision. You should understand what the role involves and choose a backup where appropriate.

What affects the cost and timing?

There is no single price that fits every estate plan, and a firm should explain its fee and scope before you hire. Cost can depend on how many documents are needed, how complex your assets and family arrangements are, and whether the lawyer is preparing a new plan or reviewing existing documents.

  • The number and type of documents, including whether a trust is appropriate.
  • The need to review real estate, business interests, accounts, or property in more than one state.
  • Questions involving blended families, a beneficiary who needs extra support, or coordination with elder law or tax concerns.
  • Whether the work includes revisions, signing guidance, or follow-up after the documents are prepared.

Timing also depends on how quickly you can provide information and make decisions, the lawyer's schedule, and whether the plan needs further review. Ask for an estimate of the steps and expected schedule, and tell the firm if you have a deadline or an urgent health or family concern.

  • When should I review the plan again, and can I contact you if my circumstances change?

Listen for answers that connect the documents to your goals instead of relying on a standard list. You should leave the conversation understanding the proposed work, the fee arrangement, and what you need to do next.

What mistakes should you avoid, and when should you call a lawyer?

  • Don't assume a will controls every account or property interest. Ask how beneficiary forms and ownership fit with the rest of the plan.
  • Don't name decision-makers without discussing the responsibility with them and considering an alternate.
  • Don't sign documents you don't understand or leave old documents in place after a major family or financial change.
  • Don't treat a plan as finished if the required signing steps have not been followed. Ask the lawyer how execution should be handled.

Call a lawyer when you are making or updating a will or trust, choosing someone to act for you, or trying to coordinate documents with property or family circumstances. It is especially sensible to seek advice when you have real estate in more than one state, a business, a blended family, or questions about long-term care and decision-making.

Kansas requirements can affect how documents are prepared and signed, and details may depend on the document and your situation. If you have moved to Kansas or own property elsewhere, ask the lawyer to check which rules apply rather than assuming an older plan works unchanged.
