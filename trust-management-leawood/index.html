What should trust management cover?

A trust is managed according to its written terms and the law that applies to it. The trustee generally handles the trust property, keeps records of income and expenses, makes decisions called for by the document, and communicates with beneficiaries as required. The exact work depends on the trust, the assets it holds, and what has happened since it was created.

A lawyer may help interpret the document, explain a trustee's responsibilities, prepare or review legal notices, and advise on questions about distributions or disputes. Financial, tax, and investment work may call for other professionals. Ask each provider to distinguish legal advice from administration or investment services so you know who is responsible for each task.

What will a local professional check first?

Before recommending a course of action, a professional needs to understand the trust and the problem the trustee is trying to solve. Bring the full trust document and any amendments, rather than only a summary or selected pages. If the trust was created in another state or holds property outside Kansas, mention that at the outset.

  • Who created the trust, who is serving as trustee, and whether a successor trustee has taken over.
  • What the trust says about distributions, decision-making, notices, and changes to the trustee.
  • What property is held in the trust and whether ownership records match the document.
  • What records are available, including account statements, expense receipts, tax filings, and prior communications.
  • Whether there is a pending distribution, disagreement, deadline, tax question, or other urgent issue.

A first review can also identify gaps in the file. It is often easier to sort out missing records early than after a beneficiary asks for an accounting or a decision is challenged.

Which provider types can help with trust management?

Trustees sometimes need one professional, and sometimes need a small team. Compare the work you need with each provider's role, experience, and limits before you hire.

  • The Eastman Law Firm: A law firm serving Leawood. Ask whether it handles the specific trust document, administration, or dispute involved; legal counsel does not automatically include bookkeeping or investment management.
  • Estate planning or trust attorney: Can explain legal duties, review trust language, and advise on disputes. The attorney may not provide ongoing asset management or accounting.
  • Bank or trust company: May offer professional fiduciary administration, recordkeeping, and asset services. Its process can be more formal, and it may have eligibility requirements or minimum account sizes.
  • Independent professional fiduciary: May take on trustee duties or administrative work, depending on qualifications and authorization. Check the person's experience, fees, insurance, and the scope of responsibility.
  • CPA or tax professional: Can help with tax filings, accounting, and financial records. This is useful for tax and reporting work, but it is not a substitute for legal advice about interpreting the trust.

Options compared by type

The right fit depends on whether you need legal advice, someone to serve as trustee, or help with records and taxes. These roles can overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

  • Legal guidance: An estate planning or trust attorney can review the document, explain duties, and advise on legal questions or disputes. Ask if the attorney handles administration work as well.
  • Trust administration: A bank, trust company, or qualified independent fiduciary may handle trustee duties and ongoing records. Compare eligibility requirements, fees, experience, and how decisions are reported to beneficiaries.
  • Tax and accounting: A CPA or tax professional can help prepare filings and organize financial records. This does not replace legal advice or necessarily include fiduciary responsibilities.
  • Combined support: Some situations call for an attorney, fiduciary, and tax professional working together. Confirm who is responsible for each task and how the providers will coordinate.

What changes the cost and timing?

There is no single price for trust management. A focused document review is different from ongoing administration, a contested distribution, or work to organize years of incomplete records. Ask for the fee structure in writing and clarify what the quoted work includes before authorizing it.

  • How many assets and accounts the trust holds, and whether ownership or beneficiary records need correction.
  • The condition of the financial records and how much time is needed to reconcile transactions.
  • Whether beneficiaries agree, are requesting information, or have raised a formal dispute.
  • Whether the work involves tax filings, property transfers, court proceedings, or professionals in other fields.
  • How quickly a decision or filing is needed and whether the relevant documents are ready.

Timing also depends on the responsiveness of banks, beneficiaries, tax professionals, and other institutions. Ask what the first milestone will be, what could hold it up, and how you will be updated if the schedule changes.

What mistakes should a trustee avoid?

A trustee should avoid treating trust property as personal property, making decisions without checking the document, or relying on memory instead of records. Even a well-intended distribution can create problems if it does not follow the trust's terms or applicable law.

Keep trust funds separate, save statements and receipts, and make a written record of important decisions. Don't promise a beneficiary a particular outcome before confirming the trustee's authority. If the trust's language is unclear or a conflict is developing, get advice before taking an irreversible step.

When should you call a professional?

Consider calling a lawyer or other qualified professional when you have just become trustee, can't tell what the trust permits, or need to respond to a beneficiary's request. It is also sensible to get help before transferring property, making a significant distribution, signing an agreement, or responding to a court notice.

A call may be especially useful when the trust was created in another state, a beneficiary disagrees with the trustee, the records are incomplete, or tax and legal questions overlap. Bring the document and a short timeline of events so the professional can quickly see what is known and what still needs checking.

  • Who will do the work, and who will be my regular contact?
  • What documents should I provide before the first meeting?
  • How are fees calculated, and what work or expenses are not included?
  • What is a reasonable first milestone, and what could delay it?
  • How do you handle communication with beneficiaries and requests for records?
  • Do you have experience with trusts involving property or parties outside Kansas?
