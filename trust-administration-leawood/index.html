What does trust administration involve?

Trust administration is the work of managing property held in a trust and following the instructions in the trust document. The exact duties depend on the document, the assets, and the circumstances, so the first step is to identify which version of the trust controls and whether there are amendments.

A successor trustee may need to locate and secure assets, review debts and expenses, keep clear financial records, provide information or notices to beneficiaries, and arrange distributions when the requirements are met. Administration can also involve tax questions, property transfers, or coordination with a probate case if some property was not held in the trust.

A trustee has legal duties to act in line with the trust and handle the property responsibly. Beneficiaries may have rights to information, but the timing and scope of what must be shared can depend on the trust terms and Kansas law.

What will a local professional check first?

An attorney will usually start by understanding who created the trust, who is serving as trustee, and what event led to administration. The attorney may review the trust and amendments, identify any co-trustees or named beneficiaries, and ask whether anyone has raised a dispute or concern.

The asset picture matters just as much as the paperwork. A professional may ask what property is titled in the trust, whether there are accounts or real estate outside it, what debts or ongoing bills exist, and whether any assets have a beneficiary designation. These details help show which tasks belong in trust administration and which may need separate attention.

  • The signed trust document and any amendments or related estate planning papers
  • A list of known trust assets, debts, regular expenses, and recent statements
  • Names and contact information for trustees, beneficiaries, and relevant financial or tax professionals
  • Any notices, court papers, tax correspondence, or written concerns already received

Which trust administration provider options should you compare?

The right fit depends on how complicated the trust is, whether people disagree, and how much support the trustee needs. Compare the provider’s role, availability, and fee arrangement rather than assuming every provider handles the same tasks.

  • The Eastman Law Firm: A local law firm with estate planning, civil law, elder law, and tax attorney offerings. Ask whether its current services include the particular trustee duties, tax coordination, or beneficiary issues involved in your matter.
  • Solo estate planning attorney: May offer direct communication with the attorney and a focused relationship. Availability and capacity for a large or contested administration can vary.
  • Larger law firm: May have several legal practice areas or staff available for a complex matter. You may want to clarify who will handle routine questions and how the work will be billed.
  • Trust company or professional fiduciary: May provide ongoing asset management or trustee services when appointed and appropriate. It may not provide the legal advice a trustee needs, so separate counsel could be necessary.
  • Tax or accounting professional: Can help with tax returns, records, and financial questions within their professional role. They generally do not replace an attorney for interpreting the trust or advising on legal duties.

What changes the cost of trust administration?

There is no reliable price to quote without knowing what the trust holds and what work is needed. A straightforward administration with organized records and no disagreements may call for less legal time than one involving multiple properties, business interests, unusual assets, or contested decisions.

Fees can also depend on whether the attorney is asked to review the trust only, guide the trustee through the full process, prepare or review notices and transfers, handle a dispute, or coordinate with tax and financial professionals. Ask whether the fee is hourly, flat for a defined task, or arranged another way, and what work falls outside the estimate.

Before hiring, request a written description of the scope, billing method, likely extra expenses, and how the firm will keep the trustee informed. A clear scope helps you compare proposals that may cover different work.

How long can trust administration take?

The timeline varies with the trust terms and the work needed to identify, value, protect, and transfer property. Delays can come from missing documents, slow responses from financial institutions, property sales, tax filings, creditor issues, or questions raised by beneficiaries.

A trustee should avoid promising a distribution date before the assets and obligations are understood. Ask the attorney which steps can move forward now, what depends on outside parties, and when the trustee should provide an update to beneficiaries.

What mistakes should a successor trustee avoid?

A trustee can create problems by treating trust property as personal property or making an early distribution before understanding the trust’s terms and outstanding obligations. Keep trust funds separate, save records, and document decisions and payments.

  • Do not assume every asset belongs to the trust or that every asset outside it automatically passes under the trust.
  • Do not sell, transfer, or distribute property without checking the trust terms and the trustee’s authority.
  • Do not ignore letters from beneficiaries, financial institutions, tax agencies, or a court.
  • Do not rely on a verbal summary of the trust when the signed document or an amendment may say something different.

If a beneficiary challenges a decision, a trustee is unsure about a conflict of interest, or an asset cannot be located or valued, get advice before taking an irreversible step. Prompt guidance can help the trustee understand the issue and keep a useful record of how it was addressed.

  • How do you charge, and what costs or services are not included?

  • What documents and asset information should I gather before work begins?
  • How do you handle disagreements among trustees or beneficiaries?
  • What Kansas or local court issues could apply if some property is outside the trust?
