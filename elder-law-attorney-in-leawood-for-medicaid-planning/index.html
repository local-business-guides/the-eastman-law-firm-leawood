What should Medicaid planning with an elder law attorney cover?

Medicaid planning starts with understanding the person's care needs, income, assets, family situation, and possible care settings. A lawyer should explain how Kansas Medicaid rules may apply, identify information that needs to be verified, and discuss lawful steps that fit the family's circumstances.

The discussion may include eligibility, asset transfers, a spouse's financial interests, powers of attorney, and how a home or other property may be treated. Planning for nursing facility care can raise different questions from planning for care at home, so ask the attorney to explain which rules and programs are relevant to your situation.

A lawyer should also be clear about the scope of the work. Advice about eligibility and asset planning is not automatically the same as preparing and filing an application, responding to state requests, or helping with an appeal.

What will a local lawyer check first?

Expect an initial review of the person's current care, likely care costs, income sources, bank and investment accounts, real estate, insurance, and any recent gifts or transfers. The attorney may also ask about a spouse, adult children, existing estate documents, and who has legal authority to act.

Bring what you have, but don't delay a consultation because a file is incomplete. A local professional can tell you which records matter most and whether additional information is needed from a financial institution, care provider, or public agency.

For a Leawood family, the key is to understand how Kansas rules apply, rather than assuming that advice from another state or a relative's past experience will fit. Rules and program details can change, so confirm current requirements with the attorney.

What affects the cost of Medicaid planning?

There is no single fee that fits every Medicaid planning matter. Cost depends on what the lawyer is asked to do and how much review is needed before giving advice.

A straightforward consultation is different from reviewing years of financial records, preparing or updating legal documents, advising a couple with shared assets, coordinating a plan with other professionals, or helping with an application or appeal. Property ownership, past transfers, urgent care needs, and missing records can add work.

Before hiring, ask for the fee arrangement in writing and have the attorney explain what it covers, what is billed separately, and what could cause the scope to change. Avoid relying on a quote until the lawyer has enough information to understand the matter.

How long can planning or an application take?

Timing depends on how quickly records can be gathered, whether existing documents need attention, and how complex the person's finances and care arrangements are. An application or agency review has its own process, so an attorney should not promise a result by a particular date without knowing the facts and current requirements.

If a move to a care setting is approaching, tell the lawyer that when you first call. Bring a timeline of recent care changes, transfers, and important notices so the attorney can identify any deadlines or urgent decisions to review.

What mistakes should families avoid?

  • Giving away money or property before checking how the transfer could affect eligibility.
  • Assuming a home, jointly held account, or other asset is automatically exempt or automatically disqualifying.
  • Waiting until a care crisis to gather financial records and legal documents.
  • Relying on advice from another state without confirming how Kansas requirements apply.
  • Assuming that a will or power of attorney alone creates Medicaid eligibility.
  • Hiring a provider without clarifying whether the fee includes advice, document preparation, an application, or follow-up with the agency.

A conversation with a lawyer before making a transfer or signing a document can help a family understand the possible consequences. If an application has already been filed or a notice has arrived, share it promptly rather than trying to guess what the response should say.

Medicaid planning checklist

  • Write down the person's care needs, likely care setting, and any upcoming decisions or deadlines.
  • Gather available records for income, bank and investment accounts, real estate, insurance, and recent transfers.
  • Collect existing wills, powers of attorney, and other estate planning documents.
  • List questions about eligibility, a spouse's finances, property, application support, and possible appeals.
  • Ask the attorney to explain the scope of work, fees, communication plan, and any steps that need prompt attention.

What questions should you ask before you hire?

  • How much of your practice involves Kansas elder law and long-term-care Medicaid planning?
  • Do you handle planning, application preparation, agency follow-up, and appeals, or only some of those services?
  • Who will be my main contact, and who will do the legal work?
  • What records should I gather before the first meeting?
  • How do you charge, what does the fee include, and what could lead to additional fees?
  • What are the main risks or open questions you see in my situation?
  • How will you keep me informed about deadlines and next steps?
  • Can you explain which Kansas rules apply and how you will confirm that they are current?

What is the 5 and 5 rule in estate planning?

The phrase often refers to a trust power that lets a beneficiary withdraw the greater of $5,000 or 5% of the trust principal in a year, under applicable rules.
