What should trust planning cover?

A trust is a legal arrangement for holding or managing property, with instructions for who benefits and when. Trust planning should start with your goals, such as arranging for someone to manage assets if you become unable to do so, planning for distributions to family, or making administration easier after death.

Ask whether the attorney will review your whole estate plan, not just prepare a trust document. A plan may also involve a will, financial power of attorney, health care documents, beneficiary designations, and instructions for transferring appropriate assets into the trust. The right documents depend on your circumstances.

What will a local attorney check?

A trust attorney should ask about your family, property, existing documents, and the people you may name as trustees or beneficiaries. They should also understand what you want the plan to accomplish and explain any trade-offs in ordinary language before recommending a structure.

For a Leawood client, ask how Kansas law applies to the documents and property involved. The attorney can explain whether those details affect the plan or call for advice in another jurisdiction.

A useful review also checks how assets will be titled and whether beneficiary forms match your intentions. A trust document alone does not automatically move property into the trust, so ask who handles funding instructions and what follow-up you’ll need to complete.

What changes the cost and timing?

There is no single fee that applies to every trust plan, and a responsible estimate depends on the work involved. The cost may change with the type of trust, the number of documents, the complexity of your assets, family circumstances, tax questions, and whether the attorney is also reviewing an existing plan.

Timing can depend on how quickly you provide information, whether you need to gather property or account records, and how many revisions are needed. Ask whether the fee is flat or hourly, what work it covers, how changes are billed, and what might lead to extra work. Request the expected timeline and next steps in writing.

Questions to ask checklist

  • What kinds of trusts do you prepare, and which goals would each option serve in my situation?
  • Who will work on my plan, and who will be my main contact?
  • What documents and advice are included in the engagement?
  • Will you review my will, powers of attorney, health care documents, and beneficiary designations as part of this work?
  • How will you explain the steps for funding the trust, and do you help with those steps?
  • How do you charge, what is included in the fee, and what could add to it?
  • How long should the work take, and what information do you need from me first?
  • How often should I review or update the plan as my family, assets, or laws change?

Listen for clear answers and a plan for follow-up, not just a promise to prepare paperwork. You should leave the conversation understanding what the attorney will do, what you must do, and how to raise questions later.

What mistakes should you avoid?

A common mistake is assuming that signing a trust document completes the plan. Ask how assets should be titled, which accounts may need beneficiary updates, and whether any property should stay outside the trust. Follow the attorney’s instructions and confirm when key steps are complete.

Avoid choosing a trust only because a friend has one, or relying on a form that does not account for your circumstances. Also be cautious about naming a trustee without considering whether that person can manage records, communicate with beneficiaries, and follow the document’s instructions.

When should you call a professional?

Consider speaking with an attorney when you own property, have children or other dependents, want to plan for incapacity, have a blended family, or need to review an older plan. A professional can also help when you’re unsure how a trust fits with your will, tax concerns, or assets in more than one state.

Bring a brief list of your goals and questions. If you already have documents, ask whether the attorney wants copies before the meeting rather than sending sensitive records through an unapproved channel.

An estate planning attorney can advise on wills, trusts, powers of attorney, and related documents. What is the 5 and 5 rule in estate planning?

The 5 and 5 rule generally refers to a trust beneficiary’s limited withdrawal power, often described as the greater of $5,000 or 5% of certain trust assets each year. It can have tax consequences and is not a standard feature that every trust needs, so ask an attorney to explain whether it applies to your plan.
