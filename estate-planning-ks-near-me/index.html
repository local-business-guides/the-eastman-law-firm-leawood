A short checklist before you meet

You don’t need to have every answer settled before asking for help. A little preparation can make your discussion more focused. Consider bringing or writing down:

  • A basic list of property and accounts you want to discuss, including your home.
  • The names of family members or other people you may want to include in your plan.
  • Any existing wills, trusts, or other planning documents you can locate.
  • Questions about who should carry out your wishes and what that role involves.
  • Recent changes in your family, finances, or plans that may affect your decisions.

If you’re unsure whether a document is relevant, bring it anyway or ask the firm what would be useful. You can also make a short list of priorities, such as providing direction for loved ones or organizing how property should be handled.

Mistakes that can leave gaps

One common problem is putting off planning because the decisions feel complicated. Waiting can leave loved ones without written guidance at a difficult time. Another is assuming that a single document covers every concern. A will, trust, and other planning choices serve different purposes, so ask how each option relates to your goals rather than choosing based on a general rule of thumb.

It can also be easy to overlook updates. A plan created before a major family or financial change may no longer match what you want. Keep a note of changes worth discussing, and ask an attorney whether your documents should be reviewed. Don’t make edits to legal documents on your own without understanding how the changes could affect the rest of your plan.

When it makes sense to call an attorney

Call when you’re ready to discuss a will or trust, have questions about probate, or want to understand how to organize your wishes. It’s also reasonable to seek advice when you’ve had a significant change in your life or simply can’t tell whether your current documents still reflect your intentions.
