What should will preparation include?

Will preparation should begin with a conversation about your family, property, and wishes. An attorney may ask who you want to receive your property, who should manage the estate, and whether you want to nominate a guardian for minor children. The plan should reflect your actual situation rather than rely on a generic form.

The attorney should also explain how the will is signed and stored, and what steps are needed to make the document valid under applicable law. A will does not automatically control every asset. Some property may pass under beneficiary designations, joint ownership, or other arrangements, so it helps to review those items alongside the will.

What will a local professional check?

A careful review includes more than names and dollar amounts. The attorney should confirm that the people named in the will are clearly identified, that the instructions are workable, and that the person responsible for administering the estate understands the role. If there are minor children, a blended family, a business, or property in more than one state, those details may affect the discussion.

The attorney should ask about major life changes, such as marriage, divorce, a new child, a death in the family, or a change in property ownership. These events can make an old will confusing or out of date. The professional should also explain which questions need a broader estate plan, rather than suggesting that a will alone covers every concern.

What changes the cost of preparing a will?

There is no single fee that applies to every will. The price can depend on whether you need a new document or a review, how involved your instructions are, and whether the work includes related estate planning documents. A straightforward set of instructions may take less attorney time than a plan involving multiple beneficiaries, family disagreements, a business, or questions about property ownership.

Before work begins, ask for the fee structure and a clear description of what it covers. Find out whether meetings, revisions, signing instructions, and follow-up questions are included, and whether extra work would be billed separately. A low initial quote is hard to compare if it covers less than another provider's proposal.

How long does will preparation take?

Timing depends on the provider's schedule and how quickly you can gather information and make decisions. The process may take longer if the attorney needs to clarify property details, review an existing document, or discuss questions involving family members or other professionals. Ask for an expected timeline and what you can do to keep the work moving.

Signing is a separate step from drafting. The attorney should explain the required signing formalities and any arrangements you need to make before you come in or complete the process. Do not assume that signing a document at home, or storing it safely, makes it valid.

What mistakes should you avoid?

  • Using a form without checking whether its wording fits your family, property, and state requirements.
  • Leaving out a backup person to serve if your first choice cannot take the role.
  • Assuming the will controls accounts or property that pass through a beneficiary designation or joint ownership.
  • Naming someone without asking whether they are willing and able to serve.
  • Putting off updates after a major family or financial change.
  • Signing without following the required formalities or understanding what witnesses must do.

These oversights can create uncertainty for the people who will carry out your wishes. If you already have a will, bring it to the consultation rather than assuming it should be replaced. An attorney can explain whether a revision, a new document, or a broader review makes sense.

What should you ask before you hire?

  • Who will meet with me and prepare or review the will?
  • What information should I gather before our first meeting?
  • What does your fee include, and what could lead to additional charges?
  • How many rounds of revisions are included?
  • Will you review beneficiary designations and property ownership as part of the work?
  • How will you explain signing requirements and document storage?
  • How long does the process usually take once you have my information?
  • What changes in my life or finances should prompt me to update the will?

Listen for clear answers about scope, communication, and what happens after the document is drafted. You should understand what the attorney will do, what remains your responsibility, and when the firm will let you know that the work is complete.

Will preparation checklist

  • Write down who you want to receive your property and who you would trust to manage your estate.
  • Gather any existing will and note changes in your family, property, or wishes since it was prepared.
  • List questions about beneficiary designations, joint ownership, and property in more than one state.
  • Ask what the fee includes, how revisions work, and what signing steps are required.
  • Confirm who will handle your matter and when you can expect the work to be completed.

When should you call a will preparation attorney?

Consider a consultation if you do not have a will, your current document no longer reflects your wishes, or you are unsure how an asset will pass. It can also be useful to speak with an attorney when you have minor children, a blended family, property outside Kansas, or concerns about who should manage your estate.

For a practical next step, write down who you want to receive your property, who you would trust to handle your affairs, and any questions about your existing documents. Then ask a local attorney to explain the process, expected timing, and fee before you decide.
